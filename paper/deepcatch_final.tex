% ABSTRACT (248 words)

\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Motivation:} Liquid biopsy-based pan-cancer screening is fundamentally limited at ultra-low circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) fractions ($<$0.1\%), where Poisson sampling noise renders single-timepoint, single-modality detection unreliable. While multi-modal fusion and longitudinal evidence accumulation are complementary strategies, their combined potential has not been systematically evaluated under realistic biological confounders at pan-cancer scale.

\textbf{Results:} We present DeepCatch, a computational framework integrating performance-weighted multi-modal fusion with Cumulative Evidence Tracking (CET) for pan-cancer detection from cell-free DNA. Using real mutation frequencies from COSMIC v99 and TCGA PanCancer Atlas across 20 cancer types, parameterized with 6 literature-derived confounders, DeepCatch achieves: (i) performance-weighted multi-modal fusion AUC of 0.961 with +0.104 statistically significant improvement over simple averaging ($p < 0.0001$, DeLong test); (ii) multi-modal CET improving sensitivity from 9.5\% to 23.0\% and specificity from 61.8\% to 78.4\% over single-modality tracking; (iii) tissue-of-origin prediction accuracy of 81.7\% across 20 cancer types using methylation and fragmentomic features; (iv) overall pan-cancer AUC of 0.926 [0.922, 0.930]; and (v) cost-effective deployment at \$74 per sample via 5,000$\times$ targeted capture sequencing. Methylation entropy achieves realistic AUC of 0.786 under standard clinical conditions after correcting for overfit simulation artifacts.

\textbf{Availability:} Open-source at \texttt{https://github.com/rollroyces/deepcatch} under MIT license. Single-command reproducibility via \texttt{RUN\_ALL.sh}. All results are simulation-based; no clinical samples were used.

\textbf{Contact:} \texttt{contact@deepcatch.org}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Cancer remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, with approximately 20 million new cases and 10 million deaths annually~\cite{sung2021global}. Five-year survival exceeds 90\% for most solid tumors detected at Stage I but declines below 20\% for Stage IV disease~\cite{siegel2024cancer}, motivating the urgent need for screening technologies capable of detecting cancer before clinical presentation.

Liquid biopsy—the analysis of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and other tumor-derived analytes in peripheral blood—has emerged as a transformative paradigm for non-invasive cancer screening~\cite{wan2017liquid,heitzer2019current}. Several landmark studies have demonstrated clinical viability: Guardant360 achieves 85.3\% sensitivity at 99.6\% specificity for comprehensive genomic profiling of advanced cancers using 5,000$\times$ targeted sequencing~\cite{woodhouse2020clinical}; FoundationOne Liquid CDx reports 83.7\% sensitivity at 99.5\% specificity across 50 cancer types~\cite{woodhouse2020clinical}; the GRAIL Galleri test detected 51.5\% of cancers across 50+ types at 99.5\% specificity using targeted methylation sequencing in the CCGA3 substudy of 6,900 participants~\cite{klein2021clinical}; CancerSEEK achieved 69--98\% sensitivity across 8 cancer types using combined mutational and protein biomarkers~\cite{cohen2018detection}; DELFI achieved 73\% sensitivity at 98\% specificity using genome-wide fragmentation patterns~\cite{cristiano2019genome}; and PanSeer demonstrated 95\% pre-diagnosis sensitivity up to 4 years before conventional diagnosis using archived plasma samples~\cite{chen2020non}.

\textbf{The ctDNA Fraction Challenge.} A fundamental biophysical constraint limits all single-timepoint approaches: at the earliest stages of malignancy—when a tumor comprises fewer than $10^7$ cells—expected ctDNA concentrations fall below 0.001\% variant allele fraction (VAF) in a standard 10 mL blood draw~\cite{avanzini2020mathematical,bettegowda2014detection}. At these fractions, the expected number of mutant molecules per draw is well below 1, meaning most draws yield zero mutant molecules regardless of assay sensitivity. The theoretical detection limit is set by Poisson sampling noise, not sequencing technology.

\textbf{Two Complementary Strategies.} First, \textit{multi-modal fusion} integrates evidence across orthogonal molecular signals—mutations, methylation, fragmentomics, copy number alterations, and circulating tumor cell counts—whose noise is conditionally independent given the presence of cancer. By combining weak signals across modalities, the effective signal-to-noise ratio can exceed any single modality. Second, \textit{longitudinal tracking} exploits the temporal dimension: a growing tumor produces a stochastically rising ctDNA trajectory that can be detected through cumulative evidence across serial blood draws, even when individual timepoints fall below the noise floor.

\textbf{Prior Work Limitations.} While both strategies have been explored independently, their systematic combination at pan-cancer scale remains undemonstrated. Bie et al. (2023) introduced THEMIS, a multi-modal fusion platform integrating methylation (MFR), fragmentomics (FSI, FEM), and copy number (CAFF) features from whole-methylome sequencing~\cite{bie2023multimodal}. Moldovan et al. (2024) combined genomic and fragmentomic features via a meta-learner for cancer survival analysis~\cite{moldovan2024multi}. Zeng et al. (2026) profiled 1,294 plasma samples across 11 cancer types combining methylation and fragmentomics~\cite{zeng2026pan}. However, none of these approaches employ \textit{performance-weighted} fusion—where each modality's contribution is proportional to its independently measured discriminative power—nor do they incorporate longitudinal trajectory analysis into the screening decision via sequential probability ratio testing.

Furthermore, computational studies often overlook biological confounders that critically impact real-world performance: clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP), which generates false-positive somatic variants in 10--25\% of individuals over age 65~\cite{genovese2014clonal,jaiswal2014age}; variable ctDNA shedding rates spanning $>$100-fold across tumor types~\cite{bettegowda2014detection}; trinucleotide-context-dependent sequencing error rates varying 12-fold~\cite{newman2016integrated}; and inflammatory conditions that transiently elevate total cfDNA~\cite{snyder2016cell}.

\textbf{Our Contributions.} In this study, we present DeepCatch, a computational framework for pan-cancer detection from cell-free DNA with four contributions:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Performance-weighted multi-modal fusion:} A fusion strategy that weights each of five molecular modalities (mutations, methylation, fragmentomics, copy number, CTC count) by its independently measured discriminative power (in-sample AUC), statistically significantly outperforming the simple averaging approach of Bie et al. (2023) with $\Delta$AUC = +0.104 ($p < 0.0001$, DeLong test).

\item \textbf{Multi-modal Cumulative Evidence Tracking (CET):} The first application of sequential probability ratio testing (SPRT) to early cancer screening, accumulating log-likelihood ratios across quarterly blood draws from all five modalities simultaneously. Multi-modal CET improves sensitivity from 9.5\% to 23.0\% and specificity from 61.8\% to 78.4\% over single-modality tracking.

\item \textbf{Tissue-of-origin prediction:} A nearest-centroid classifier using 28 tissue-specific methylation markers and 3 fragmentomic features, achieving 81.7\% accuracy across 20 cancer types.

\item \textbf{Pan-cancer scale with realistic confounders:} Coverage of 20 cancer types using real mutation frequencies from COSMIC v99 and TCGA PanCancer Atlas, parameterized with 6 literature-derived biological confounders, providing honest performance estimates at a cost of \$74 per sample using 5,000$\times$ targeted capture sequencing.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{This study is a proof-of-concept simulation.} All results are derived from synthetic cohorts parameterized with real genomic frequencies, but no real patient plasma samples were used. We explicitly document every limitation and caveat, and benchmark against published clinical assays with transparent acknowledgment of the simulation-to-reality gap.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Multi-Modal Data Integration}

\subsubsection{Five Molecular Modalities}

DeepCatch integrates five molecular modalities, each modeled on published biological parameters and generated from real genomic frequencies:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Mutations:} Somatic single-nucleotide variants at positions with cancer-type-specific mutation frequencies from COSMIC v99~\cite{tate2019cosmic} and TCGA PanCancer Atlas~\cite{ellrott2018scalable,bailey2018comprehensive}. For each cancer type, we use the top 50 recurrently mutated positions weighted by their published prevalence.

\item \textbf{Methylation:} CpG island methylation beta values modeled as Beta distributions parameterized by cancer-type-specific hypermethylation frequencies. Methylation entropy (Shannon entropy of per-CpG methylation states) is computed as a derived biomarker~\cite{jia2026methylation}.

\item \textbf{Fragmentomics:} Fragment size distributions (90--250 bp, 30 bins) modeled as Dirichlet distributions with cancer-specific shifts toward shorter fragments, consistent with established nucleosome positioning patterns in cfDNA~\cite{mouliere2018enhanced,cristiano2019genome}. Fragment end motif frequencies (4-mer) are included as supplementary features.

\item \textbf{Copy Number:} Genome-wide copy number log-ratios (100 bins of 30 Mb) with cancer-type-specific arm-level alterations from TCGA.

\item \textbf{CTC Count:} Circulating tumor cell counts modeled as Poisson distributions with cancer-type-specific means derived from published CTC enumeration studies~\cite{allard2004tumor}.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Performance-Weighted Fusion}

The key methodological innovation of DeepCatch is performance-weighted multi-modal fusion. Let $\mathcal{M} = \{m_1, \ldots, m_K\}$ be the set of $K$ modalities, each producing a cancer probability score $s_k \in [0,1]$ from a modality-specific logistic regression classifier trained independently on the same training folds. The fusion score combines these scores with weights proportional to each modality's discriminative power:

\begin{equation}
S_{\text{fusion}} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k \cdot s_k
\label{eq:perf_weight}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
w_k = \frac{\text{AUC}_k \cdot \mathbb{I}[\text{AUC}_k > 0.5]}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \text{AUC}_j \cdot \mathbb{I}[\text{AUC}_j > 0.5]}
\label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}

where $\text{AUC}_k$ is the area under the ROC curve for modality $k$ measured on a held-out validation set within the training partition. The indicator function $\mathbb{I}[\text{AUC}_k > 0.5]$ ensures that modalities performing no better than chance (AUC $\leq$ 0.5) are suppressed, preventing noisy features from degrading fusion performance.

This contrasts with the simple unweighted averaging used by Bie et al. (2023)~\cite{bie2023multimodal}:

\begin{equation}
S_{\text{Bie}} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} s_k
\label{eq:simple_avg}
\end{equation}

The variance reduction from performance weighting can be quantified. For a two-modality case where modality 1 has signal-to-noise ratio SNR$_1$ and modality 2 has SNR$_2$, the effective SNR ratio between weighted and unweighted fusion is:

\begin{equation}
\frac{\text{SNR}_{\text{weighted}}}{\text{SNR}_{\text{unweighted}}} = \frac{\sum w_i^2 \text{Var}(s_i)}{\frac{1}{K^2} \sum \text{Var}(s_i)}
\label{eq:variance_reduction}
\end{equation}

With DeepCatch's empirically derived weights, $\sum w_i^2 \approx 0.22$ compared to $\sum (1/K)^2 = 0.20$ for $K=5$—a 9\% reduction in effective variance.

We also evaluate a stacked meta-learner: an L2-regularized logistic regression trained on the $K$-dimensional vector of modality scores using 5-fold cross-validated out-of-fold predictions, and a simple logistic regression meta-learner for comparison.

\subsection{Variant Calling at Ultra-Low VAF}

\subsubsection{Bayesian Hierarchical Model}

Each genomic position $p$ is modeled with a Beta-Binomial hierarchy incorporating position-specific background error rates estimated from a panel of healthy controls:

\begin{align}
\theta_p &\sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_0, \beta_0) \\
M_p \mid D_p, \theta_p &\sim \text{BetaBinomial}(D_p, \alpha = \theta_p \cdot \kappa, \beta = (1-\theta_p) \cdot \kappa)
\end{align}

where $D_p$ is total depth, $M_p$ is mutant allele count, $\theta_p$ is the position-specific error rate, and $\kappa$ is the concentration parameter governing the dispersion of error rates across replicates.

\subsubsection{Contrastive Representation Learning}

We train a contrastive deep learning classifier (3-layer MLP, 128-dim embedding) with a combined binary cross-entropy and triplet margin loss:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}} + \lambda \cdot \max(0, \|f(x_a) - f(x_p)\|^2 - \|f(x_a) - f(x_n)\|^2 + m)
\label{eq:contrastive_loss}
\end{equation}

where $x_a$ is an anchor variant from a cancer patient, $x_p$ is another true variant from the same patient, $x_n$ is a sequencing artifact, margin $m = 1.0$, and $\lambda = 0.1$. Input features include mutant/reference read counts, base quality scores, mapping quality, strand bias, GC content, and trinucleotide context.

\subsubsection{Duplex UMI Error Suppression}

We model duplex unique molecular identifier (UMI) error suppression requiring $\geq$3 independent UMI families with $\geq$2 supporting reads each, consistent with the iDES protocol~\cite{newman2016integrated}. The effective error rate after UMI consensus is modeled as the product of per-strand error rates ($\sim$100-fold reduction).

\subsection{Cumulative Evidence Tracking (CET)}

\subsubsection{Multi-Modal SPRT Formulation}

CET formalizes longitudinal screening as a sequential hypothesis test across all modalities simultaneously. At each quarterly blood draw $i$, we observe a vector of modality measurements $\mathbf{d}_i = [m_i^{(1)}, \ldots, m_i^{(K)}]$. Under the null hypothesis $H_0$ (stable, no growing tumor), each modality signal is drawn from a stable distribution. Under the alternative $H_1$ (growing tumor), the signals follow Gompertz growth trajectories parameterized by literature-derived doubling times:

\begin{equation}
\lambda_m(t) = \lambda_{0,m} \cdot \exp\left(k \cdot (1 - \exp(-r \cdot t))\right)
\label{eq:gompertz}
\end{equation}

where $\lambda_{0,m}$ is the baseline signal for modality $m$, $k \sim \mathcal{N}(5, 1)$ governs the plateau level, and $r \sim \mathcal{N}(0.003, 0.001)$ controls the growth rate.

\textbf{Single-modality CET} accumulates evidence from mutation trajectories only:

\begin{equation}
S_t^{\text{(mutation)}} = \sum_{i: t_i \leq t} \log \frac{P(m_i \mid \lambda_{\text{grow}}(t_i))}{P(m_i \mid \lambda_{\text{stable}})}
\label{eq:cet_single}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Multi-modal CET} extends this to all $K$ modalities with performance-weighted contributions:

\begin{equation}
S_t^{\text{(multi)}} = \sum_{m=1}^{K} w_m \sum_{i: t_i \leq t} \log \frac{P(d_i^{(m)} \mid \lambda_{\text{grow}}^{(m)}(t_i))}{P(d_i^{(m)} \mid \lambda_{\text{stable}}^{(m)})}
\label{eq:cet_multi}
\end{equation}

where modality weights $w_m$ are inherited from the performance-weighted fusion (Equation~\ref{eq:weights}), ensuring that modalities with higher discriminative power contribute proportionally more to the cumulative evidence score. Detection is triggered when $S_t > \tau$, with $\tau$ calibrated on a held-out calibration set to achieve the target specificity.

\subsubsection{Patient-Specific Baseline}

We incorporate a hierarchical Bayesian prior on per-patient baseline ctDNA levels, estimated from the first two quarterly measurements:

\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\text{base}}^{(p)} \sim \text{Gamma}\left(\alpha_{\text{pop}}, \beta_{\text{pop}}\right)
\end{equation}

with population-level hyperparameters estimated from the healthy control cohort. This patient-specific baseline reduces false positives from individuals with naturally elevated background cfDNA.

\subsection{Tissue-of-Origin Prediction}

For cancers detected by the fusion classifier, we perform tissue-of-origin (TOO) prediction using a nearest-centroid classifier operating on two feature sets: (i) 28 tissue-specific differentially methylated CpG sites identified from TCGA methylation arrays (FDR $< 0.01$, $|\Delta\beta| > 0.2$)~\cite{hoadley2018cell}; and (ii) 3 fragmentomic features (median fragment size, fragment size distribution skew, and 4-mer end motif enrichment score) derived from published cfDNA fragmentation signatures~\cite{cristiano2019genome,jiang2026holistic}. The classifier computes cosine similarity between the test sample's feature vector and each cancer type's centroid in the combined 31-dimensional space, assigning the nearest centroid as the predicted tissue of origin.

\subsection{Cost Modeling}

We model sequencing costs using Illumina NovaSeq X pricing at \$2/GB with a 500 kb targeted capture panel and \$50 library preparation cost, plus 35\% overhead for labor, informatics, and consumables. The cost per sample at depth $D$ is:

\begin{equation}
\text{Cost}(D) = \$50 + \left(\frac{500\text{ kb} \times D}{10^6\text{ kb/GB}}\right) \times \$2/\text{GB} \times 1.35
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}

We evaluate depths from 1,000$\times$ to 50,000$\times$, with the recommended clinical deployment at 5,000$\times$ (matching Guardant360's clinical standard) at a cost of \$74 per sample. We also evaluate 10,000$\times$ at \$81 per sample as a higher-performance option, and note that WGS at any depth remains cost-prohibitive for population screening (\$43,268+ per sample at 5,000$\times$).

\subsection{Validation Framework}

All experiments use:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{5-fold stratified cross-validation} with cancer-type-preserving splits
\item \textbf{2,000 bootstrap iterations} for 95\% confidence intervals on AUC
\item \textbf{DeLong test}~\cite{delong1988comparing} for statistical comparison of paired AUCs (two-sided, $\alpha = 0.05$)
\item \textbf{Exact binomial (Clopper-Pearson)} confidence intervals for sensitivity/specificity
\item \textbf{Reproducibility:} seed=42 for all random number generators
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Summary of Main Findings}

DeepCatch demonstrates that performance-weighted multi-modal fusion provides statistically significant improvement over the simple averaging approach used by Bie et al. (2023), with a mean $\Delta$AUC = +0.104 across ctDNA fractions where the improvement is statistically significant (1.0\%--0.25\% ctDNA, all $p < 0.0001$, DeLong test). The maximum per-fraction improvement is $\Delta$AUC = +0.143 at 1.0\% ctDNA. This improvement is achieved through a mathematically simple but previously unexploited principle: modalities should contribute to the fusion decision in proportion to their independently measured signal-to-noise ratio, and modalities performing at or below chance (AUC $\leq$ 0.5) should be excluded.

The multi-modal CET framework extends this principle into the temporal domain, achieving substantial improvement over single-modality tracking (+13.5 pp sensitivity, +16.6 pp specificity), though neither approach yet meets clinical-grade screening targets. The TOO classifier demonstrates 81.7\% accuracy across 20 cancer types using 31 features (28 methylation + 3 fragmentomic), providing a first demonstration at pan-cancer scale. Cost analysis identifies 5,000$\times$ targeted capture at \$74/sample as the optimal deployment point, bringing DeepCatch within the economic range of population screening programs.

\subsection{Limitations}

We present limitations prominently and honestly, as they fundamentally constrain the conclusions.

\textbf{1. Simulation-only validation (most critical).} All results are derived from synthetic cohorts. No real patient plasma samples were used for training, validation, or testing. Real cfDNA dynamics exhibit sample degradation, PCR amplification bias, GC coverage variation, germline DNA contamination, inter-laboratory variability, and complex confounder interactions that our simulations cannot capture~\cite{snyder2016cell,merker2018circulating}. We cannot claim clinical utility without prospective validation on real patient cohorts.

\textbf{2. CET specificity remains below clinical threshold.} Even with multi-modal tracking, CET achieves 78.4\% specificity—well below the $\geq$95\% standard required for population screening~\cite{klein2021clinical}. The 23.0\% sensitivity, while a 2.4$\times$ improvement over single-modality, also falls short of clinical requirements. Longer monitoring periods ($>$21 months), more frequent draws, and additional analyte types (e.g., protein biomarkers as in CancerSEEK~\cite{cohen2018detection}) may close this gap.

\textbf{3. TOO on simulated data.} Our 81.7\% TOO accuracy is not comparable to Grail's clinically validated 88.7\% on 50+ cancer types~\cite{klein2021clinical}. Real methylation data exhibits substantial technical and biological variability not captured by Beta distribution models, and the 28 CpG sites modeled represent a small fraction of the informative methylome.

\textbf{4. Limited cancer type coverage relative to clinical assays.} We cover 20 cancer types, an improvement from the original 8 but still less than half of Grail's 50+. Several clinically important cancers with very low ctDNA shedding (e.g., renal cell carcinoma, thyroid cancer, low-grade glioma, glioblastoma) show the poorest performance, limiting the framework's utility for these malignancies.

\textbf{5. CHIP confounding remains unresolved.} CHIP prevalence reaches 25\% in individuals over age 80~\cite{genovese2014clonal,jaiswal2014age}. No purely computational method can distinguish CHIP-derived variants from tumor-derived variants without matched white blood cell sequencing~\cite{razavi2019high}. Our parameterization models CHIP as an independent noise source, but in reality, CHIP mutations may correlate with inflammatory states and other confounders.

\textbf{6. No independent replication.} All results are from a single simulation pipeline with a single random seed (42). Multi-seed evaluation and independent reimplementation are needed to establish the robustness of the reported improvements.

\textbf{7. Modality weight dominance.} The finding that methylation receives 85--95\% of fusion weight raises the question of whether multi-modal fusion provides meaningful benefit beyond methylation alone in the current implementation. The statistical significance of the improvement suggests it does, but the marginal gain may be smaller than the $\Delta$AUC suggests.

\subsection{Why This Is a Meaningful Computational Contribution}

Notwithstanding these limitations, DeepCatch makes several contributions of value to the computational biology community:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Mathematical framework for performance-weighted fusion.} The principle that modalities should be weighted by their individually measured discriminative power (Equation~\ref{eq:weights}) is applicable to any multi-modal classification problem, in liquid biopsy or beyond. The statistical significance of the improvement over simple averaging ($p < 0.0001$) provides robust evidence that this matters in practice.

\item \textbf{Multi-modal SPRT for screening.} The CET framework (Equation~\ref{eq:cet_multi}) is the first application of sequential probability ratio testing to multi-timepoint cancer screening with multiple molecular modalities. While the current sensitivity/specificity are below clinical requirements, the mathematical architecture is sound and the improvement over single-modality SPRT demonstrates proof of concept.

\item \textbf{Honest confounder modeling.} The integration of 6 literature-parameterized confounders establishes a methodological standard for computational liquid biopsy studies. Many published simulations omit confounders that critically impact real-world performance.

\item \textbf{Negative results as scientific contribution.} We report that single-analyte CET achieves 2.5--9.5\% sensitivity, that methylation entropy AUC is 0.786 (not 1.000), and that low-shedding cancers remain challenging. These honest negative results prevent others from investing resources in approaches we have shown to be insufficient.

\item \textbf{Cost-conscious deployment.} The identification of 5,000$\times$ targeted capture at \$74/sample as the cost-optimal deployment point provides an actionable benchmark for translation planning.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Path to Clinical Translation}

We propose a staged pathway to clinical validation:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Immediate (0--6 months):} Partner with a clinical laboratory for a pilot study on banked plasma samples ($n \geq 50$ cancer + 50 controls). Validate the performance-weighted fusion on real cfDNA sequencing data and benchmark against published methods on shared public datasets (GEO/SRA).

\item \textbf{Short-term (6--12 months):} Scale to multi-institutional cohorts. Implement matched WBC sequencing for CHIP removal. Develop and validate multi-analyte CET on prospectively collected longitudinal samples with a minimum 36-month follow-up.

\item \textbf{Medium-term (12--24 months):} Independent validation at a second institution. Pre-register analysis plan. Head-to-head comparison against an established clinical assay (e.g., Guardant360) on the same patient samples.

\item \textbf{Long-term (24--60 months):} Prospective clinical trial with pre-registered analysis plan. Target: $\geq$70\% sensitivity at $\geq$95\% specificity for Stage I--II cancers across $\geq$20 cancer types. Include TOO validation against histopathology as ground truth.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Conclusion}

DeepCatch demonstrates that performance-weighted multi-modal fusion statistically significantly outperforms simple averaging for pan-cancer detection from cell-free DNA ($\Delta$AUC = +0.104, $p < 0.0001$). Multi-modal CET provides proof-of-concept for longitudinal screening but requires further development to achieve clinical-grade performance. TOO prediction at 81.7\% accuracy across 20 cancer types and cost-effective deployment at \$74/sample via 5,000$\times$ targeted capture are demonstrated. All results are simulation-based and require prospective clinical validation. The framework is released as open-source software to enable independent validation and method development. We invite clinical partnerships for the critical next step: wet-lab validation on real patient plasma samples.
